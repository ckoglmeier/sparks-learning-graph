# Added concepts

Ten original conceptual claims extend the pinned upstream graph. Each has a parent prompt, observable evidence, three teaching activities, diagnostics, source references and explicit model limits. Technical sources support correctness; the proposed activities are not evaluated interventions. New ages are proposed guidance through age 12.

## Comparing density using equal volumes

`mt_915b9dc0b3714097` · proposed ages 9–12

Compare the density of uniform materials by comparing masses of equal volumes, and explain why changing the amount of the same uniform material does not by itself change its density. Distinguish total mass from mass relative to occupied volume; no density formula or particle theory is required.

**Why add it:** Material Properties Vocabulary names density but does not assess relational comparison; The Particle Model requires a different microscopic explanatory model. Existing mass and volume topics measure separate quantities, not their relationship. This is a distinct conceptual claim, not an easier copy of particle theory.

**Example diagnostic:** Two equal-volume blocks A and B have masses 20 g and 40 g. A larger block C made from the same uniform material as A has twice its volume and mass. Rank the material densities and explain whether cutting B into two equal pieces changes its material density.

**Limits:** Use uniform samples under unchanged conditions. Do not infer hollow-vehicle behaviour from material density alone. Density is outside the NGSS* 5-PS1-3 assessment boundary; no such alignment is authored.

**Sources:** [American Chemical Society, Inquiry in Action, Grade 5 Lesson 2.4, objectives, key concepts and NGSS boundary](https://www.acs.org/education/resources/k-8/inquiryinaction/fifth-grade/substances-have-characteristic-properties/lesson-2-4--density-and-sinking-and-floating.html); [Hardy, Jonen, Möller and Stern (2006), Journal of Educational Psychology 98(2), 307–326; DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.98.2.307; abstract, method and discussion](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/didaktik_des_sachunterrichts/dokumente/literaturmoeller/article.pdf).

## Water displaced by immersed objects

`mt_92fa2c4720f84f79` · proposed ages 9–12

Explain that a water-excluding object displaces a volume of water equal to the volume of the part below the water surface, and use this to compare controlled partial and full immersion cases. Distinguish displaced volume from object mass and from water-level height; no density calculation is required.

**Why add it:** What volume means concerns unit-cube volume, and ordinary-pouring conservation concerns unchanged liquid amount. Neither assesses the relation between immersed object volume and displaced liquid. The new claim does not duplicate measuring an irregular object by subtraction as a procedure.

**Example diagnostic:** Two sealed rigid blocks have the same outside volume but different masses. Both are held fully underwater in turn. Compare the displaced volumes. Then lower one block deeper without changing its shape. Does displacement increase? Explain, and contrast with lowering a half-immersed block to full immersion.

**Limits:** Use rigid sealed or water-excluding objects; explicitly account for flooded openings. Exclude absorption, dissolution, trapped bubbles changing volume, spill loss and appreciable fluid compressibility. Do not confuse displacement with buoyant force or pressure.

**Sources:** [American Chemical Society, Middle School Chemistry, Lesson 3.2, key concepts and summary](https://www.acs.org/middleschoolchemistry/lessonplans/chapter3/lesson2.html); [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 14.4, Buoyant Force and Archimedes Principle](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/14-4-archimedes-principle-and-buoyancy); [Hardy, Jonen, Möller and Stern (2006), Journal of Educational Psychology 98(2), 307–326; DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.98.2.307; abstract, method and discussion](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/didaktik_des_sachunterrichts/dokumente/literaturmoeller/article.pdf).

## Buoyant support and floating

`mt_0124c7499b7c49de` · proposed ages 9–12

Explain floating and initial rising or sinking using upward support from displaced water and downward weight. A freely floating object at rest displaces water with the same weight as the object; a fully immersed object released from rest initially rises or sinks according to the balance. Apply the model to hollow hulls and changed loads without requiring equations or a microscopic pressure derivation.

**Why add it:** Forces Vocabulary names upthrust; density vocabulary and particle explanations do not establish the relation between water displacement, support and load. This claim integrates a single flotation model and retains pressure mechanism as an optional explanation, not a required whole bundled pressure topic.

**Example diagnostic:** A watertight bowl floats at rest. The same material and mass pressed into a compact lump sinks. Explain both using weight and displaced water. Predict what happens to the bowl waterline when cargo is added, and explain whether the sinking lump still receives upward support.

**Limits:** Distinguish steady floating from moving objects and terminal-speed cases. Use water-excluding hull geometry; do not assert every hollow object floats. No surface-tension-supported tiny objects or changing fluid density in the core task.

**Sources:** [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 14.4, Buoyant Force and Archimedes Principle](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/14-4-archimedes-principle-and-buoyancy); [Hardy, Jonen, Möller and Stern (2006), Journal of Educational Psychology 98(2), 307–326; DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.98.2.307; abstract, method and discussion](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/didaktik_des_sachunterrichts/dokumente/literaturmoeller/article.pdf); [American Chemical Society, Inquiry in Action, Grade 5 Lesson 2.4, objectives, key concepts and NGSS boundary](https://www.acs.org/education/resources/k-8/inquiryinaction/fifth-grade/substances-have-characteristic-properties/lesson-2-4--density-and-sinking-and-floating.html).

## Pressure in still liquids

`mt_b39a1788b3424357` · proposed ages 10–12

Explain that pressure in a connected liquid at rest acts on immersed surfaces in all orientations and increases with depth below the free surface. Compare pressures at equal and different depths in the same liquid under the same surface pressure; distinguish local pressure from net buoyant force without calculating hydrostatic equations.

**Why add it:** Deformation & Fluid Pressure bundles this concept with elastic/inelastic deformation and thermal expansion. The new focused claim extracts independently assessable hydrostatics without changing or retiring the older broader claim or transferring its mastery. Moments, Pressure & Hooke's Law also includes independent content and cannot substitute.

**Example diagnostic:** In connected still water with a common open surface, sensors in a wide and a narrow region are at the same depth. Compare their pressure. Move one deeper and compare again. Explain whether the liquid also pushes on vertical walls and why moving a rigid fully submerged box deeper does not by itself increase displaced volume.

**Limits:** Same liquid density, rest, shared surface pressure and negligible compressibility in core comparisons. Use identical sensor areas; no pressure calculations, gas laws or decompression advice. Pressure-force differences explain support, but the older deformation/thermal claim is not awarded by this task.

**Sources:** [NOAA National Ocean Service, How does pressure change with ocean depth?](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/pressure.html); [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, section 14.4, Buoyant Force and Archimedes Principle](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/14-4-archimedes-principle-and-buoyancy).

## Sensing and acting in physical control systems

`mt_da7df960e3c3497f` · proposed ages 8–12

Explain the causal roles of a sensor or input device, a controller following a rule, and an actuator or output in a supplied physical control system. Trace information separately from the power that enables an action, and distinguish sensing a condition from deciding and acting on it; no circuit-building or program-writing procedure is required.

**Why add it:** Parts of a computer classifies/selects peripherals and Real-World Robots identifies examples. Decisions and input in programs implements branching. This claim assesses the causal physical chain and power-versus-information distinction, independently of writing code.

**Example diagnostic:** A distance sensor reports 2 units. Controller A runs a motor when the reading is below 3; controller B stops it under that condition. Both have the same sensor and battery. Predict both outputs, identify who senses/decides/acts, and explain why a disconnected motor power supply can prevent movement even with a valid reading.

**Limits:** State whether the program polls, handles events or uses pre-supplied readings. Do not claim an if block continuously monitors input or that all physical sensors stop sensing between reads. Use supplied safe low-voltage or simulated systems; this claim does not certify wiring competence.

**Sources:** [Micro:bit Educational Foundation, First lessons with MakeCode and the micro:bit, lessons 1–5](https://www.microbit.org/teach/lessons/first-lessons-with-makecode-and-the-microbit/); [Department for Education, National curriculum in England: Computing, Key Stage 2](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-computing-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-computing-programmes-of-study); [Department for Education, National curriculum in England: Design and technology, Key Stage 2 technical knowledge](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-design-and-technology-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-design-and-technology-programmes-of-study).

## Correcting a system using feedback

`mt_84988393c9e342de` · proposed ages 10–12

Explain how repeatedly measuring an outcome, comparing it with a target or target band and choosing a corrective action forms a feedback loop. Distinguish outcome feedback from a fixed timed action or an unrelated sensor trigger, and predict how a bounded controller responds to a changed load or disturbance without promising perfect control.

**Why add it:** Loops and input decisions implement execution structures but do not require the measured output to feed back into a regulating action. A thermostat-style causal loop is independently explainable from code construction; do not create separate rover/submarine feedback nodes.

**Example diagnostic:** A tank controller reads the water level each cycle. Below a target band it opens an inlet; above it, it closes the inlet. A separate drain can remove water. Compare this with opening the inlet for a fixed time. Explain a response when draining increases, identify the feedback path, and explain why a door sensor playing a tune is not automatically door-position regulation.

**Limits:** Use a supplied plant model, bounded disturbance and actuator limits. Core claim is conceptual; PID equations, stability proofs and code writing are separate. For submarine activities, account for inertia and drag; neutral buoyancy does not immediately remove vertical speed.

**Sources:** [Åström and Murray, Feedback Systems, author-hosted Introduction, chapter summary](https://fbswiki.org/wiki/index.php/Introduction); [National Centre for Computing Education, Year 5 Starting with selection, public lesson description and objectives](https://teachcomputing.org/curriculum/key-stage-2/programming-a-selection-in-physical-computing/starting-with-selection); [Micro:bit Educational Foundation, First lessons with MakeCode and the micro:bit, lessons 1–5](https://www.microbit.org/teach/lessons/first-lessons-with-makecode-and-the-microbit/).

## Force and travel in simple machines

`mt_d1f48410cd8b4254` · proposed ages 8–12

Explain the tradeoff between input force and input travel when a low-loss simple machine performs the same output task slowly. Use supplied motion comparisons to explain why reducing input force requires more input travel, and distinguish this from reducing the energy required. Transfer the relationship across a ramp, a wheel-and-axle lifting device, and supplied wedge or screw models without requiring mechanical-advantage formulae.

**Why add it:** Simple machine arrangements and everyday uses assesses recognition and function. Levers, Pulleys & Gears assesses force-changing mechanisms but does not require the reciprocal input-travel explanation. Efficiency, Sankey diagrams, and work done requires numerical efficiency and diagrams. This qualitative energy/travel relationship is independently assessable; separate topic copies for each tool are unnecessary.

**Example diagnostic:** Two ideal ramps raise the same box to the same height slowly; one ramp is longer. Which needs the smaller push and which longer input travel? In a separate ideal crank lift, the handle radius doubles while the drum and load stay fixed. Compare hand travel, required tangential force and load rise per turn. Explain why neither machine creates energy.

**Limits:** Quasi-static low-loss comparisons; fixed output load/displacement. No formal work calculations, Sankey diagrams or proof of practical construction competence. A cutting wedge may also break material; do not assume its actual work equals a lifting task.

**Sources:** [OpenStax Physics, section 9.3 Simple Machines, input/output travel, ideal advantage and efficiency](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/9-3-simple-machines); [Department for Education, National curriculum in England: Science, Year 5 Forces and KS3 Motion and forces](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study).

## Load placement and static tipping

`mt_9401faebe1804335` · proposed ages 8–12

Predict whether a rigid supported object begins to tip under gravity using the vertical line through its combined centre of gravity and its base of support. Explain how changing load position, support width or centre height affects the static tipping margin in supplied models. Do not infer dynamic vehicle safety or require numerical moments.

**Why add it:** Balanced & Unbalanced Forces concerns investigation of net force and motion, not the support-region condition for tipping. Moments, Pressure & Hooke's Law bundles numerical torque, hydraulics and elasticity. The proposed claim is the spatial static-stability criterion, with supplied combined centre markers, not an easier replacement for the whole older claim.

**Example diagnostic:** A rigid model has two supporting feet and a marked combined centre of gravity. Its downward vertical line is initially inside the support region. A load is moved outward without changing total mass until the line passes beyond the right support. Predict the change, explain a counterweight solution and describe the exactly-at-edge case.

**Limits:** Rigid body, gravity and support forces; quasi-static tilt and no sliding. Provide combined centre markers; calculating centre of mass is not required. No claim of real vehicle, crane or human balancing safety.

**Sources:** [OpenStax College Physics 2e, section 9.3 Stability, base of support and centre of gravity](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/9-3-stability); [Department for Education, National curriculum in England: Design and technology, Key Stage 2 technical knowledge](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-design-and-technology-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-design-and-technology-programmes-of-study).

## Contact force spread over an area

`mt_56c39fbf4ef341a0` · proposed ages 8–12

Explain average contact pressure qualitatively by comparing normal force and the area over which it is spread. Predict the direction of change when one factor is held fixed, and recognise when changing both factors requires more information. Apply controlled comparisons to indentation in the same yielding surface without treating pressure as identical to total force.

**Why add it:** Deformation & Fluid Pressure covers elastic change, thermal expansion and fluid depth. Moments, Pressure & Hooke's Law covers other bundled quantitative claims. Neither assesses the controlled solid-contact force/area comparison; force vocabulary alone is insufficient.

**Example diagnostic:** The same block is placed gently on a broad face and a narrow face on an identical yielding surface. Compare its total downward force and average contact pressure. Then compare a wider pad carrying an unknown heavier load with a narrow pad carrying a lighter load: can width alone settle the pressure ranking?

**Limits:** Average normal contact pressure, not local stress concentrations. Controlled material, loading time and surface condition for indentation comparisons. No formal pressure units/calculation or soil-bearing engineering claim.

**Sources:** [OpenStax College Physics 2e, section 11.3 Pressure, definition and contrasting contact areas](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure); [Department for Education, National curriculum in England: Science, Year 5 Forces and KS3 Motion and forces](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study).

## Solar-panel orientation and light capture

`mt_3568068de7804719` · proposed ages 8–12

Explain why the orientation of a fixed-area photovoltaic panel relative to direct incoming light can change the light it intercepts and its electrical output. Compare controlled orientations and distinguish facing the current light direction from a fixed tilt chosen for energy over a day or year; no semiconductor theory, trigonometry or installation design is required.

**Why add it:** Sunlight warms things up concerns thermal effects. Building an energy-converting device is a design/test procedure, and Energy stores and transfers classifies stores/pathways. None assesses geometric interception by a photovoltaic surface; successful device construction alone does not demonstrate that explanation.

**Example diagnostic:** Two identical panels under the same direct beam have identical circuits and temperatures. One faces the beam square-on and the other nearly edge-on. Predict which captures more direct light and generally supplies more power under suitable operation. If the light direction moves, must the old best orientation stay best? Does more power at one instant establish the greatest energy over a whole day?

**Limits:** Qualitative direct-beam comparison with identical panel, circuit/load and temperature. Diffuse light, shade and electrical operating limits require additional information. No mains wiring, installation recommendation or exact cosine-power law claim.

**Sources:** [US Energy Information Administration, Solar photovoltaic output depends on orientation, tilt, and tracking, 19 November 2014](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=18871).
